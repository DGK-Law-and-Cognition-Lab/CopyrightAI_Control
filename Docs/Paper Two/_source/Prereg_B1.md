## Study information

**Title.** Prompting, the work after the prompt, and lay authorship (Paper B, Study 1).

**Date.** 6 November 2026

**Research question.** When an AI image generator makes an image from a person's prompt, which of the person's acts make the image his in ordinary judgment: prompting it rather than finding it, hours that bought no choices, choosing one of about 100 outputs, revising the prompt, or revising that the generator did on its own? The image is held fixed; only the story of how it came about changes. This study opens Paper B, on which of a person's acts make him the author of an image that something else carried out.

The U.S. Copyright Office (the Office) would refuse registration in every case, on two premises. The first is that "given current generally available technology, prompts alone do not provide sufficient human control to make users of an AI system the authors of the output" (*Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2: Copyrightability*, Jan. 2025, "the report," p. 18). The second is that time, regenerating, choosing and revising are not grounds for authorship, and that automation strengthens the refusal (pp. 19 to 21). Experimental jurisprudence uses empirical methods to inform questions of jurisprudence and legal theory (Tobia, 2022); in that frame, this study asks whether lay judgment shares the two premises, without saying what the rule should be.

The study also tests, with predictions fixed in advance, scoped credit: the principle that people credit a person for what they take that person to have done. Paper One, our five studies of AI labels (Kamper, Lin & Holyoak, submitted, *Beyond the Original Sin: How People Judge a Person's Contribution to Artworks Built on Artificial Intelligence*), stated the principle after seeing its results. We state it here as one rule: Alex is credited with an aspect of what is seen to the extent that an act the description attributes to him produced that aspect. Scoped credit and the Office's sources predict different results for three comparisons: prompting against finding, choosing, and revising.

**Contrasts.** Each trial walks through one image under one of six stories (cells), which differ only in who wrote and typed in the prompt and in what happened afterward, including the time it took; every story says that the image matches the prompt and that Alex kept it.

| Cell | What happened | Stated time |
|---|---|---|
| o, found | Another artist wrote the prompt and typed it in; Alex found the image online | About half an hour, most of it saving the file |
| a, prompt only | Alex typed in his prompt; one image | About half an hour, most of it setting the generator up |
| r, routine hours | As in a | About 10 hours, most of it fixing a generator that kept crashing |
| b, re-roll | About 100 images from one prompt; Alex kept the best match to his prompt | About 10 hours |
| c, revise | Alex rewrote his prompt about 100 times, an image after each, and kept the best match to his first prompt | About 10 hours |
| d, tool iterates | The generator rewrote the prompt on its own about 100 times and picked the best match while Alex was away | About 10 hours of the generator's time |

The primary outcome is the authorship share, the 0-to-100 answer to "How much of this image is Alex's own creative work?" Each contrast is a difference between two cell means, in points:

- PR, prompting against finding: a minus o.
- A, delegating the iteration to the tool: c minus d.
- H, hours alone: r minus a.
- Sel, choosing among outputs at equal hours: b minus r.
- R, revising against regenerating, at equal hours and numbers of versions, with a choice in both: c minus b.

Each contrast takes one of four states under the state rule (Inference criteria): positive, negative, zero (shown to lie within ±8 points, the smallest difference that counts as a difference), or undetermined. A family is the set of contrasts that answers one hypothesis. An account is a claim about what lay credit follows, written as the states it allows on each contrast of its family. Rival accounts allow patterns that do not overlap, so no result supports two; a compatible account overlaps a rival and is reported only as consistent with the result or contradicted by it.

**H1, prompting and delegation.** The states of PR and A match the pattern of one account. We do not predict which.

| Account | What credit follows | PR | A | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Neither prompting nor iterating earns credit | Credit needs more than a prompt, and delegating the iteration costs nothing | zero | zero | Rival |
| Work after the prompt counts; prompting does not | Labor, choosing or revising by Alex earns credit; a prompt alone does not | zero | positive | Rival |
| Initiator credit | Starting the work earns credit, and the credit survives the machine's later work | positive | zero | Rival |
| Scoped credit | Each act of Alex's that produced what is seen | positive | positive | Rival |
| Office practice | Refuse in every cell; its reasoning on automation ranks d below c | zero | zero or positive | Compatible with the first two rows |

PR is the direct test of the first premise. Initiator credit is separated from the Office by PR, not by A, because both allow A to be zero.

**H2, work after the prompt.** The states of H, Sel and R match the pattern of one account. We do not predict which.

| Account | What credit follows | H | Sel | R | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Office practice | None of these acts | zero | zero | zero | Rival |
| Time | The hours Alex spends | positive | zero | zero | Rival |
| Selection | Alex choosing among outputs | zero | positive | zero | Rival |
| Revision | Alex shaping the output by revising | zero | zero | positive | Rival |
| Scoped credit | Acts that produced what is seen: choosing and revising, not routine time | zero | positive | positive | Rival |
| Work | Creative work of any kind: hours, choosing and revising all add work | positive | positive | positive | Rival |

**Nonclaims.**

1. PR also changes who owned the image (Alex's own prompted output in cell a, another artist's in cell o), so a positive PR does not separate prompting as an act from a belief that Alex owns what he prompted.
2. A bundles who revises, who chooses, and Alex's own hours. It tests whether credit survives when the tool does all three, not which one matters.
3. H is a test of labor only when the effort check (effort in cell r against cell a; Analysis plan) is positive; otherwise routine hours did not read as work, and labor was not tested for routine time.
4. Choosing one of about 100 outputs is selection of a single output, not the creative selection, coordination or arrangement of AI material that the Office accepts (report, p. iii), which is not tested here.

## Design plan and sampling plan

**Study type.** Randomized experiment, conducted online. Its one manipulated factor, cell, varies within participants: each person sees every cell, each on a different image. Composition, which of two images of each scene a list shows, varies across lists only to balance the images over the cells.

**Design.** Two trials per cell: twelve trials, each on a different scene. Each participant gets one of 24 lists, which fix which scene carries which cell. With the scenes ordered SB-P1, SB-N1, SB-P2, SB-N2, and so on to SB-N6 (P with a person, N without), list k (0 to 23) uses rotation q = k modulo 12 (the remainder after dividing k by 12) and composition V1 (each scene's first image) if k is below 12, I2 (its second image, with the main elements moved) otherwise. In rotation q, list position j (0 to 11) shows the scene at position (j + q) modulo 12, and cell i (numbering o, a, r, b, c, d from 0 to 5) occupies positions 2i and 2i + 1. Each cell thus falls once on a scene with a person and once without, and across the 24 lists every scene appears in every cell four times. Within a scene and composition, every cell shows the same file. Only an AI image generator is described, because revising a camera's setup would itself be control under the Office's reasoning about photographs.

Each person sees all six cells, and comparison across trials can sharpen differences that people would not notice when judging one case alone. The Office judges one application at a time, so the estimate from each person's first trial (safeguard 2) is the closest analogue of its situation. Five safeguards limit comparison and check for its effect.

1. No one sees an image under two descriptions, and the lists rotate which scene carries which cell.
2. First-trial check: every confirmatory contrast is also estimated on each participant's first presented trial alone, where each person contributes one cell, so cells are compared across people. If that estimate's 90 percent confidence interval (a range built to contain the true value in 90 percent of repeated samples) excludes the full-model estimate, the contrast is reported as dependent on comparison across trials. With one sixth of the sample per cell (16 to 24 participants at the candidate targets; Rationale), this check detects only large distortions.
3. Trial order is random, and every trial changes the scene as well as the description, so no one contrast stands out.
4. A purpose probe at the end asks what the study was mainly about, and every confirmatory family is rerun without those who choose the true purpose.
5. No statement of the Office's rule precedes the trials; the "would" item and the knowledge quiz, which point to or state the law, come only after the last trial.

**Materials.** Twelve photographic scenes, each with a four-sentence plan (subject, arrangement, light, colors; 27 to 36 words) that participants read as the prompt Alex, or in cell o another artist, wrote. SB-P1's plan is: "A woman reads in an armchair beside a tall window. She holds the open book on her lap. Rain streaks the glass, and the light is soft and grey. Soft blues, greys, and cream." The other eleven plans, the unseen placement sentences and the fixed realism line every prompt adds are in the prompt bank, the file of every prompt used to make the images, posted with this registration. Six scenes include a person and six do not; the feature that could differ from a plan by chance is the weather in five scenes, an animal in three, a light in two and a passer-by in two. Each scene has two files, made by script with Nano Banana Pro (`gemini-3-pro-image`, a Google image model) at its own 3:2 size of 1264 by 848 pixels, with the seed (the number that fixes a model's random starting point) of candidate n set to 1000 + n. Each prompt is the plan, then a realism line ("An unretouched documentary photograph taken with an ordinary digital camera in available light: true-to-life, slightly muted colors, fine sensor noise, natural imperfections and everyday clutter. Not cinematic, not HDR, no dramatic lighting, no glossy commercial look."), then "No text, watermark, or border." We chose this model and line by eye from a side-by-side comparison of six models, each with and without the realism line, after judging an earlier set made with another model obviously AI-made; that set was set aside before any participant took part. V1 is generated from the plan; I2 from the plan plus a placement sentence that moves the main elements, the elements that sentence names (such as the woman and the armchair), to the other side of the frame at the other scale, worked out from where the object detector (below) found them in V1. The plans name no placement, so both match.

Each file had to pass four kinds of automatic check. The object detector, OWLv2 (`google/owlv2-base-patch16-ensemble`, revision `cfd3195ba4ea`), takes a short text such as "a dog" and returns boxes around matching objects, each with a score from 0 to 1; it had to find every main element with a score at or above the score cut (set below). The image-description model, GPT-5.1 (version `gpt-5.1-2025-11-13`), asked one yes-or-no question at a time, had to answer "yes" to "Does the image show" followed by the feature phrase of plan version 1, for example "Does the image show rain streaking the glass?"; "yes" to "Is the image free of any visible text, letters, numbers, signatures or watermarks?"; and "yes" to 'Does this photograph fit the following description: "..."?' for each of the plan's first three sentences (subject, arrangement, light), so that every file shows what its plan says. In the animal and passer-by scenes the detector also had to find the animal (SB-P2 a dog, SB-N2 a cat, SB-N4 a sparrow) or the passer-by (SB-P6 a jogger apart from the girl), and in the kitchen scene (SB-P4) it had to find no second person. Weather and light are not objects a detector can see, so in those scenes the detector confirmed the main elements and the description model the feature. I2 also had to have its main elements on the side and at the scale its placement sentence names: the centre of the box around them on the named side of the image's middle, and the share of the frame that box covers below the scale cut for "small" or at or above it for "large". A rule written before any image was made set both thresholds from the first candidate of each scene's V1, frozen before any acceptance decision: the score cut, 0.29, is the midpoint between the median best score of the main elements (0.503) and the highest score the detector gave to five animals named in no plan (0.071); the scale cut is the median share of the frame covered by the main elements' box (0.155) rounded to two decimals, 0.16. The script first rounded twice and gave 0.15; this was corrected to the written rule before any participant took part, and the files that decision touched were searched again in candidate order.

Candidates are numbered in the order made and the first to pass is kept, so no one picks images. The check that each file fit its plan's first three sentences was added before any participant took part, after a check of every plan sentence showed that some accepted files missed part of their plan; the files that failed it were searched again in candidate order. After 20 failed candidates a file gets one recorded sentence added to its prompt, naming the check that failed most often, and 20 more tries; a scene that still fails is replaced by a reserve scene of its kind, written in advance to the same rules. Four files needed that sentence (the I2 of SB-P1, SB-P5 and SB-N4, and SB-N3's V1). SB-N3, the night street, failed after it: in all 40 tries its I2 showed shop signs or other text, and usually more than one car where the plan has one, so the reserve scene without a person, SB-R2 (the pier, a weather feature), took its place in every list. The candidate number, prompt and every check result of each file, the thresholds, every amendment with its date, and each plan's word count are in the manifest posted with this registration. Asked afterwards whether each accepted file fits each sentence of its plan, colors included (96 checks), the description model said yes 93 times; the three exceptions are the color sentence of SB-N4's two files and, on a repeated question, the first sentence of SB-P1's V1, which it had confirmed when that file was judged, so the model is not fully stable on a borderline image. Every file appears equally often in all six cells across lists, so any mismatch is the same in every cell and cannot bias a contrast.

**Procedure.** Consent; the captcha trap (Data collection); primer and comprehension items; the study's story; twelve trials in random order, with an instructed-response item after the third ("To show that you are reading each question, choose 'Somewhat disagree' here," answered strongly disagree / disagree / somewhat disagree / neither agree nor disagree / somewhat agree / agree / strongly agree; only "Somewhat disagree" passes); story recall, right after the last trial; the "would" block; end items; debriefing. The primer, the story, each trial's pages, the registration decision and the debriefing are shown one sentence per line. The primer is the same in every study of Paper B and of Paper A (our companion studies of how people divide an image built on material Alex did not make):

> In this study you will see images and read how they were made. A person can apply to the U.S. Copyright Office to register a work as their own. The Office reviews each application and decides whether to register the work. Registering a work does not sell it. It creates a public record that names the work's author. You will be asked what the Office should do with some applications.

Three comprehension items follow, true or false, each testing a sentence of the primer, with one retry: "The U.S. Copyright Office decides whether to register a work." (True); "Registering a work with the Copyright Office means selling it." (False); "In this study you will be asked what the Copyright Office should do with some applications." (True). An attempt fails if any of the three is answered wrongly, and the retry shows the primer again; two failures end the session before any manipulated text. The story:

> Alex is a digital artist. He makes images with an artificial intelligence (AI) image generator, a program that makes an image from a written description called a prompt. The generator makes a different image each time it runs, even from the same prompt. For most images, Alex writes his own prompt. For some, he uses an image that another artist made and posted online. He never changes an image by hand. Before each image you will be told how it was made. For each image, you will also be asked to explain your answer.

Each trial walks through its image on four pages, so that participants take in the prompt, and then what was done with it, before any question. The first page shows the prompt, the scene's plan, in a box below "**Alex** wrote this prompt for the next image:" (cells a to d) or "**Another artist** wrote this prompt for the next image:" (cell o), for at least 5 seconds. The second tells what was done, in the order it happened, one sentence per line, with the image below it, labeled "The image Alex kept," for at least 8 seconds from the moment the image loads. The third shows the image and, below it, the prompt, under the heading "The prompt Alex wrote:" or "The prompt another artist wrote:", and what was done, in a column that stays in view, with the trial's items (Measured, items 1 to 7) beside it; it has no minimum time. The fourth, laid out the same way, asks the written reason (item 8). Every trial has these four pages and no others. What was done, the same for every scene:

- o: "Another artist typed the prompt into an AI image generator, and it made an image. The image matched the prompt on the first try, so the artist posted it online for anyone to use. Alex found the image, saved it and backed up the file. This took him about half an hour in all. He kept the image."
- a: "Alex typed his prompt into the AI image generator, and it made an image. The image matched his prompt on the first try, so he did not need to change anything. He saved the image and backed up the file. This took him about half an hour in all. He kept the image."
- r: "Alex typed his prompt into the AI image generator, but it crashed before making an image. He restarted it and tried the same prompt again, and it crashed again. This happened again and again until it finally worked. Fixing these problems took him about 10 hours. The image it made matched his prompt, so he kept it."
- b: "Alex typed his prompt into the AI image generator, and it made an image. It did not quite match his prompt, so he had the generator make another from the same prompt, without changing any words. He did this about 100 times, looking at each new image. This took him about 10 hours. He kept the image that best matched his prompt."
- c: "Alex typed his prompt into the AI image generator, and it made an image. It did not quite match his prompt, so he rewrote some of the words and had the generator make a new image. He did this about 100 times, looking at each new image before rewriting again. This took him about 10 hours. He kept the image that best matched his first prompt."
- d: "Alex typed his prompt into the AI image generator, and it made an image. It did not quite match his prompt, so the generator rewrote some of the words itself and made a new image. It did this about 100 times on its own, while Alex was away. This took the generator about 10 hours. It picked the image that best matched Alex's prompt, and Alex kept it."

Statements compared by a contrast differ by at most five words (o 58, a 53, r 57, b 62, c 66, d 68), and none uses the word "effort." Each tells what happened in order, so that in cell c Alex rewrites the prompt after seeing each image, in cell b he reruns the same prompt after seeing each image, and in cell d the generator does the rewriting; the iterating cells say why the first image was not kept (it "did not quite match"), and every cell ends with an image that matches the prompt. In cell r the 10 hours go to work on making the image that involves no choice about how it looks, fixing a generator that keeps crashing before it makes an image; in cell a the image comes on the first try. In cell o the other artist posts the image "for anyone to use," so that taking it is not read as theft.

Right after the last trial comes story recall (End items, item 1). Then the "would" block: an introductory page reads "Here are two images you saw earlier. We have one more question about each." The two designated trials (Randomization) are then shown again, in the order they were first seen, each on one page with the image and what was done and with no minimum viewing time, with the line "This time, the question is what the Office would actually do, not what it should do.", this item, the registration options in the same order, and then the confidence item:

> Under current U.S. law, what do you think the Copyright Office would actually do?

The debriefing reads:

> Thank you for taking part. This study asks which of a person's acts make an image from an AI image generator their own work: writing the prompt, spending time on it, picking one of many images, or rewriting the prompt. The U.S. Copyright Office says that, with today's AI image generators, none of these acts makes a person the author of the image. Your answers help test whether people judge this way. Alex and his stories were invented for this study, and every image was made with an AI image generator.

A line with the researchers' contact details follows.

**Randomization.** Each participant's list comes from the survey code that SONA (the subject pool's sign-up system) assigns: the open lists, taken in order of k, are numbered from 0, and the participant gets the one numbered code modulo the number of open lists. A script counts analyzable participants (those not excluded; Exclusions) per list once a day and closes full lists, so no person reads the data. The registration options appear in the order whole, part, refuse; part, refuse, whole; or refuse, whole, part, as floor(code / 24) (the code divided by 24, rounded down) modulo 3 is 0, 1 or 2. Trial order is randomized in the page. The written reason is asked on every trial. In list k, the "would" block shows again the trials at list position 2i of cell i, for i = k modulo 6 and i = (k + 3) modulo 6, so each cell is shown again in 8 of the 24 lists. Story recall asks about the last trial shown, so its cell is set by the random trial order. The order of the purpose probe's first four options and the order of the six believability items are randomized in the page. Every assignment is recorded.

**Blinding.** Participants are not told that others see the same images under other descriptions, or what is predicted. Written reasons are scored only by the fixed word lists of Paper One's automatic text scoring; no person and no language model reads them. Analysis code is written and tested on simulated data before the data are opened.

**Existing data.** None. The image model was chosen by an automatic comparison of model outputs that involved no participants.

**Data collection.** The UCLA Psychology subject pool, through SONA, for course credit, in UCLA's Fall 2026 quarter, under IRB #24-001292 (UCLA's institutional review board; principal investigator Holyoak). Pages run on Pavlovia (which hosts them and stores the data) from our template, which asks participants to confirm that they are using a desktop or laptop computer and ends the session before consent if they are not (the device type the browser reports is recorded), and includes a captcha trap (a test that ends sessions run by software). The trap comes right after consent, so a session it ends is saved and credited, like every session that ends early (participants who close the page are credited by hand in SONA); a participant who declines consent saves no data and is sent to the credit link. Each person takes part once across Paper One, every Paper A and Paper B study, and Prompt Position (our studies of prompts entered into AI image chains), enforced by SONA settings that hide this study from earlier participants and from those in studies listed in the same quarter, and by checks of earlier codes. The session is planned at about 39 minutes and posted as 45: twelve trials, each with its prompt page, what was done, question page and written reason, about 26.6 minutes; story recall and the "would" block, about 1.1; fixed parts, about 11.3 (consent, primer and comprehension, story, instructed-response item, end items with the believability items and the two scales, and debriefing).

The first 40 analyzable participants form an internal pilot, a check of the materials against three gates, each computed by script.

1. Belief: at least 70 percent answer "Yes" or "Partly" to the belief item; on failure, the story and the six statements are revised.
2. Recall: at least 80 percent answer the story-recall item correctly, counted over every complete session so far that meets the other exclusion rules, since recall failures are excluded from the 40; on failure, the six statements and the recall item are revised.
3. Timing: the median session is at most 45 minutes, the posted length. If not, the listing pauses and control, then the "would" block, are removed for later participants until the median, less the measured time of the removed items, fits. The effort item is kept, because it decides how H is read (Nonclaim 3).

If a gate other than timing fails, every participant run so far is dropped and barred, the change is recorded as a deviation from this registration, those sessions count against the 1,200 sessions budgeted for Papers A and B, outside this study's cap, and recruitment resumes with the revised materials. If only the timing gate fails, the 40 stay in the sample: control comes after the share on its trial, and the "would" block after the last trial, so removing them changes no confirmatory answer already given. If every gate passes and nothing changes, the 40 stay in the sample.

**Sample size.** 96 analyzable participants, the same number in each of the 24 lists: the smallest candidate sample that meets the criteria (Rationale). Recruitment is capped at 119 sessions, which allows for an assumed exclusion rate of 15 percent and for the excess expected from closing full lists once a day; the cap is this study's share of the 1,200 SONA sessions budgeted for Papers A and B. If SONA cannot supply the target by the end date, the fallback sample is 72 analyzable participants, one fewer per list.

**Rationale.** Because support needs every contrast of a family in an allowed state, the whole decision rule was simulated: datasets were generated from each rival account, the registered model was fitted to each by the registered fitting rule (Primary model), and each was classified by the state rule. Authorship share had a standard deviation of 28 points, with 62 percent of its variance from participants, 6 percent from scenes and 32 percent residual (the partition of Paper One's Study 1 effort ratings under the AI label, an assumption for this measure). Participants' sensitivity to each cell had a standard deviation of 7 points (15 in a sensitivity run), and scenes' sensitivity to each cell (scene-by-cell variation) 0 or 2.8 points, 0.10 of the share's standard deviation. Contrasts an account calls nonzero were set to 12 points (1.5 times the 8-point bound) and, separately, to 8; those it calls zero, to 0. The criteria are that the generating account is supported in at least .80 of datasets without scene-by-cell variation and .70 with it, and that no rival is supported in more than .05; the compatible Office row of H1 is not a rival. Four candidate samples were simulated, 72, 96, 120 and 144 analyzable participants (3 to 6 per list), each in the two main settings (nonzero contrasts at 12 points and a participant-sensitivity standard deviation of 7, without and with scene-by-cell variation), with 500 datasets per account and setting. The target is the smallest candidate of at least 96 (4 per list) at which both families meet the criteria in both main settings, and the fallback is the candidate below it. If a family cannot meet the criterion with scene-by-cell variation at any candidate, because variation across scenes sets a ceiling that more participants barely raise, its part of this rule is met at the smallest candidate whose rate with that variation is within .02 of its rate at 144, and the family is reported as underpowered for its zero states; the bound is never widened. Confirmation runs at the target then used 1,000 datasets per account in each main setting and 500 in each sensitivity and bound setting, and the runs at the fallback used 500. At the target, the lowest rate at which a family supported its generating account was .95 without scene-by-cell variation and .84 with it, and no rival exceeded .02. With a participant-sensitivity standard deviation of 15, the lowest rate was .52; with nonzero contrasts at the bound itself, where a contrast sits on the edge of no difference, it was .78; in these runs no rival exceeded .05. At the fallback, the lowest rates were .85 and .64, and no rival exceeded .03; the second family therefore falls short of the .70 criterion at the fallback when scenes vary. The simulation code (`sim_B1.R` with `sim_common.R`, and `smallest_sample.py`, which runs the candidate search), its full tables and their SHA-256 checksums (fingerprints that change when a file changes; sim_B1.R d3ba8b02d9d005ab7d3092139e40c3a6b9bcd4e9afeadb228ceb5c0ffd14a4f6; sim_common.R 26158be94c207ae9ab1c12103fe0fcd7a7720cbe56ee2f34e5b5a30b2800d275 (the B1 tables were produced with an earlier version of this file that lacked two options for logistic models, which B1 does not use; rerunning B1 with the posted version gives identical results); B1_sim.csv e31569a0e40a65cc5c03096c38e394c59814829c9edbe6f07f5afb8006be02f0; smallest_sample.py 242a05b16a51b3e1a218dc8401c6e3993d678856cdac3f4f2e3841e8f771e009; B1_search.csv 22da0dd051bb6336020b27983d78b2a1c09e159810e97b251aee2f38a6114ad3) are posted with this registration.

**Stopping rule.** Recruitment stops when every list has its share of the target in analyzable participants (the target divided by 24), when recruited sessions reach the cap, or on the end date, 11 December 2026, the last day of UCLA's Fall 2026 quarter, whichever comes first. If the end date comes first, recruitment stops at the achieved sample, which must reach the fallback: a list below its share of the fallback (the fallback divided by 24) stays open into UCLA's Winter 2027 quarter until it reaches that share, recruited sessions reach the cap, or that quarter ends, whichever comes first. The simulated support rates at the fallback are then reported. Lists may end slightly unequal; their counts are reported.

## Variables

**Manipulated.**

- Cell, within participants: o, a, r, b, c and d, two trials each.
- Composition, for balance only: V1 or I2, fixed for a list and so balanced across cells.
- Stated hours, part of each cell's description: about half an hour in cells o and a; about 10 in cells r, b, c and d, the same in all four so that Sel and R compare equal hours.

**Measured, in order of presentation.** On every trial (every slider shows no handle until clicked, so there is no starting value to anchor on):

1. Registration decision: "Alex applies to the U.S. Copyright Office to register this image as his own work, naming himself as its author. His application describes how it was made, as you have just read. What should the Office do?" Options: "Register the whole image as Alex's work"; "Register only part of the image as Alex's work, and leave out the rest"; "Refuse to register it."
2. Confidence: "How confident are you in that answer?" Slider from 0 (not at all) to 100 (completely).
3. Authorship share, primary: "How much of this image is Alex's own creative work?" Slider from 0 (none of it) to 100 (all of it).
4. Control: "How much did Alex's own choices determine how this image looks?" Slider from 0 (not at all) to 100 (completely).
5. Aspect items: "Which parts of this image are Alex's own creative work?" "What the image shows"; "Where things sit in the picture"; "The colors and the light." Each is answered Yes, No or Not sure on its own row. The prompts name the subject, the light and the colors but never where things sit in the picture, which the generator chose.
6. Effort, the check on hours (it shows whether the stated hours read as work): "How much effort went into making this image?" 1 None at all; 2 A little; 3 A moderate amount; 4 A lot; 5 A great deal (the amount labels of Paper A's creativity items, in place of Paper One's, whose fourth and fifth labels participants found hard to tell apart).
7. Creativity, worded after *Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co.*, 499 U.S. 340, 345 (1991): "How much creativity went into making this image?" with Paper One's definition below the question, as in Paper One: "Creativity is defined as the presence of at least a minimal original, intellectual contribution to the creation of the work." 1 None at all; 2 A little; 3 A moderate amount; 4 A lot; 5 A great deal. It rates how much creativity went into the work, not whether the legal threshold is met.
8. Written reason, on every trial, on its own page after the question page. Above the answer box, a box quotes the participant's registration decision on that trial, "You chose:" followed by the chosen option in quotation marks. The question reads "In a sentence or two, why did you choose this answer about what the Office should do?" Free text, required, with pasting disabled. Asked last, it cannot change its own trial's answers. Explaining an answer can make later answers more deliberate, but the reason is asked on every trial, in every cell, so it cannot by itself create a contrast.

After the last trial, before the "would" block:

1. Story recall, about the last trial shown: a page headed "One more question about the image you just saw." shows its image again, without the prompt or what was done, with "How was this image made?" (Another artist made the image, and Alex found it online / In about half an hour, the generator made one image from Alex's prompt / Alex spent about 10 hours fixing the generator, and then it made one image / The generator made about 100 images from the same prompt, and Alex picked one / Alex rewrote his prompt about 100 times and picked one image / The generator rewrote the prompt on its own and picked one image / I don't remember). The correct answer is the trial's cell; "I don't remember" counts as wrong.

End items, after the "would" block, in order:

2. Belief: "Did you believe the stories of how each image was made?" (Yes / Partly / No).
3. Purpose probe: "Which of these do you think the study was mainly about?" "Whether the way an AI image was made changes credit" (the true purpose); "Whether image quality affects liking"; "Whether people can tell AI images from photographs"; "Whether longer prompts earn more credit"; "Something else." The first four appear in random order, "Something else" last.
4. Believability, one item per cell, listed here in the order o, a, r, b, c, d and shown in random order: "How believable were the stories about images that Alex found online?"; "... that the generator made from Alex's prompt in about half an hour?"; "... for which Alex spent about 10 hours fixing the generator?"; "... for which the generator made about 100 images from the same prompt?"; "... for which Alex rewrote his prompt about 100 times?"; "... for which the generator rewrote the prompt on its own?" Each from 1 (not at all believable) to 7 (completely believable). They are recorded and reported by cell; they never gate and never exclude.
5. Experience: "How often have you made images with an AI image generator?" (Never / Once or twice / Sometimes / Often); "Apart from casual snapshots, do you take photographs?" (No / Sometimes / Often / Professionally); "Do you draw or paint?" (No / Sometimes / Often / Professionally).

The wording, the instructions for participants and the item order are those of the full scale's ready-to-use appendix (Schepman & Rodway, 2023), restricted to the ten items. There are no right or wrong answers. We are interested in your personal views." 7. ## Analysis plan

**Software.** R, with lme4 for mixed models, lmerTest for tests and intervals with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom (an approximation suited to mixed models), emmeans and marginaleffects for contrasts, and brms (a package for Bayesian mixed models) for robustness models. A mixed model gives each participant and each scene its own baseline (a random intercept) and its own sensitivity to a factor (a random slope), so that conclusions extend to images like these and not only to these images (Judd, Westfall & Kenny, 2012).

**Primary model (H1, H2).** A linear mixed model regresses share on composition (V1 minus 0.5, I2 plus 0.5) and on five Helmert codes for cell, h1 to h5, each of which compares one cell with the average of the cells before it. With the cells in the order o, a, r, b, c, d, code hj is minus 1 for the first j cells, j for cell j + 1 and 0 for the rest (the columns of R's contr.helmert(6)). Each registered contrast is a fixed linear combination of the code coefficients; PR, for example, is twice the h1 coefficient. Participants and scenes each get a random intercept and uncorrelated random slopes for the five codes, which two trials per cell make estimable; composition, a balancing factor, gets no slope. In lme4 notation: `share ~ h1 + h2 + h3 + h4 + h5 + composition + (1 + h1 + h2 + h3 + h4 + h5 || participant) + (1 + h1 + h2 + h3 + h4 + h5 || scene)`. Models are fitted with lme4's default settings. A fit fails when it stops with an error, fails to converge (the optimizer, the routine that searches for the estimates, returns a nonzero code, or lme4 or lmerTest warns that the model failed to converge) or is singular (a random-effect variance is estimated at zero); after a failed fit one term is removed and the model refitted, in this order: interaction slopes, then the slope with the smallest estimated variance in the failed fit, then the scene intercept. The model left after the last removal is accepted. This model has no interaction slopes. Ties, and fits that stop with an error and so give no variance estimates, remove the first remaining slope in the order scene h5 to h1, then participant h5 to h1. Each contrast's 90 percent confidence interval and two-sided p-value use the t distribution with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom.

**Inference criteria.** The equivalence bound B, the smallest difference that counts as a difference (Lakens, Scheel & Isager, 2018), is ±8 points. That is 0.30 of the planning standard deviation of one share answer (28 points), the standardized size our Prompt Position Study 1 registration uses, and it is the same in every Paper A and Paper B study so that results can be compared; results also give it in units of the standard deviation of share observed in Paper A's Study 1. Each contrast takes exactly one state, in this order (the state rule):

1. Zero, if its 90 percent confidence interval lies inside ±B. Zero takes precedence, so an effect smaller than B counts as zero even when significant.
2. Positive or negative, if its two-sided p-value (the probability of a difference at least this large if the true difference were zero) is below .05.
3. Undetermined, otherwise. An undetermined contrast is never read as zero.

An account is supported when every contrast in its family lands in a state it allows, contradicted when any lands in a signed or zero state it excludes, and inconclusive otherwise. The full pattern is reported even when it matches no account. No correction for multiple tests is applied, because support needs a full pattern and no single contrast can confer it; the error that matters, a wrong account being supported, is held at or below .05 by the simulation (Rationale).

**H1.** Decided by the states of PR and A against the H1 table; a positive PR is read with Nonclaim 1.

**H2.** Decided by the states of H, Sel and R against the H2 table, with H read as Nonclaim 3 says. H positive with the effort check positive means people credit hours that bought nothing, the "sweat of the brow" (labor without creative choices) that *Feist* rejected (499 U.S. at 352 to 54).

**Effort check (secondary).** Effort in cell r minus effort in cell a, from a linear mixed model of the effort item with the primary model's terms and fitting rule, judged by the state rule with Paper One's bound of ±0.224 points on the 1-to-5 scale (half of Paper One's Study 1 creativity penalty).

**Answer keys.** Every cell's key is "Refuse to register it," on the "should" and "would" items alike, and every aspect item's key is "No": the tool generated every part of the image, and an idea is never protected (17 U.S.C. § 102(b)). Cell o: the image is another person's prompted output, and Alex is not its author (17 U.S.C. § 201(a)). Cell a: "given current generally available technology, prompts alone do not provide sufficient human control" (report, p. 18). Cell r: "copyright protects original authorship, not hard work or 'sweat of the brow'" (p. 20). Cell b: that identical prompts can generate multiple different outputs "further indicates a lack of human control" (p. 20), and, in a commenter's words the report adopts, "selection of a single output is not itself a creative act" (p. 21). Cell c: revising and resubmitting prompts is "'re-rolling' the dice" (p. 20). Cell d: the lack of control is "even clearer in the case of generative AI systems that modify or rewrite prompts internally" (p. 19), and "increased automation and optimization may bolster our current conclusions" (p. 21). No key is left open by these sources.

The keys are fixed to these sources as they stand on the filing date; a later change in Office guidance is reported, not used to rescore. The Office's positions are agency policy, and the report says that "There may come a time when prompts can sufficiently control expressive elements in AI-generated outputs to reflect human authorship" (p. 21). On 6 November 2026, the filing date, the Office had issued no registration guidance, report part or appeals-board decision on AI material since 12 May 2026, when the Register of Copyrights, who heads the Office, testified that it planned further guidance on disclaimer practice within the year (testimony at 4 n.25); that guidance had not issued. Two challenges to the Office's refusals were pending without a ruling. In *Allen v. Perlmutter*, No. 1:24-cv-02665 (D. Colo.), cross-motions for summary judgment (each side asks for judgment without a trial) were fully briefed on 27 February 2026. In *Suryast U.S. Enterprises, LLC v. Perlmutter*, No. 2:26-cv-04999 (C.D. Cal.), the company filed an amended complaint on 28 October 2026, and the court then denied as moot the government's motion to dismiss, which had argued that the suit was filed in the wrong court and that the company has no right to bring it (improper venue and lack of standing) (29 October 2026). *Thaler v. Perlmutter* is final; the Supreme Court declined review on 2 March 2026 (No. 25-449). The Office's AI page and both dockets were rechecked on 6 November 2026, and their status is stated as of that date. Agreement with the keys is reported cell by cell, with no single agreement coefficient.

**Secondary analyses.** Each is labeled secondary, judged at .05 without correction, and changes no confirmatory verdict.

- Registration decision: the five contrasts on the probability of not refusing (either registration option coded 1, so that positive means more credit), from a logistic mixed model (a mixed model for a yes-or-no answer) with the primary model's terms and fitting rule. Each is a difference in predicted probability averaged over the fitted normal distribution of the participant and scene random effects that apply to each cell, with their variances held at their estimates; it has a delta-method interval (a standard approximation for the uncertainty of a transformed estimate) and a bound of ±0.10. Both families' accounts are also classified on these states.
- Effort, control and creativity: the five contrasts on each, from linear mixed models with the primary model's terms and fitting rule, given states by the state rule with bounds of ±0.224 points for effort and creativity and ±8 points for control. Control shows whether people infer control from revising.
- Each aspect item: the five contrasts, coded 1 for "Yes" and 0 otherwise, from logistic mixed models estimated as for the registration decision, with a bound of ±0.10; "Not sure" is also reported on its own, by cell.
- "Should" against "would": agreement with the key on each item, on the two reshown trials, by cell, with the limit that each "would" answer follows a "should" answer and a written reason on the same image, which push the two together.
- Believability: the six end items, reported by cell; they never gate and never exclude.

**Exclusions.** A participant is excluded for any of the following:

- failing the three comprehension items twice;
- failing the captcha trap or the instructed-response item;
- finishing in less than one third of the median completion time of all complete sessions in the final data set (the daily list-closing script uses the median of the sessions so far only to decide which lists close);
- any earlier participation in Paper One, a Paper A or Paper B study, or Prompt Position;
- a later session by a SONA code that has already started a session of this study;
- a wrong answer to the story-recall item.

Belief, the believability items, the purpose probe and the effort check never exclude.

**Intention to treat.** Cell varies within participants, so exclusions cannot differ by cell. As a robustness check that changes no confirmatory state, the primary model is repeated on every participant with a complete session, whatever their exclusion status, except internal-pilot participants dropped after a failed gate, and reported beside the primary result.

**Missing data.** Responses are required, so sessions are complete or incomplete. Incomplete sessions are excluded and replaced by continued recruitment within the stopping rule.

**Robustness.** Each check is reported beside the primary result and changes no confirmatory state.

- A zero-one-inflated beta model, a regression for proportions that allows answers of exactly 0 and 100 (Smithson & Verkuilen, 2006; Bürkner, 2017), is fitted to share in brms with the design terms and random intercepts for participant and scene on all three of its parts, and the contrasts are recomputed on the expected share. Effort and creativity are refitted as ordinal mixed models, which treat answers as ordered categories (Bürkner & Vuorre, 2019).
- A claim of no difference must also hold among participants who answered "Yes" to the belief item; a zero state that fails there is reported as zero in the full sample only, and an account that needs it as supported in the full sample only.
- First-trial check (Design, safeguard 2): share on each participant's first presented trial is regressed on the five cell codes and composition, with a random intercept for scene; if that fit fails by the fitting rule, the same fixed terms are fitted by ordinary regression.
- Purpose probe: both families are refitted without participants who chose "Whether the way an AI image was made changes credit," and their states and classifications are reported.

**Exploratory.** Moderation of each contrast by the experience items, the two Short GAAIS-10 scores, anthropocentric creativity beliefs, copyright knowledge and belief; differences between scenes with and without a person; the share of participants choosing each purpose-probe option; and word-list flags in the written reasons, by cell.
